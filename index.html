Experiment 1 — Safety Rules & Lab Equipment

Personal & housekeeping safety rules, emergency equipment, and the glassware you'll use all semester

Personal Safety Rules

Goggles Worn at ALL times — no exceptions. Contact lens wearers need extra caution.
Lab Coat Mandatory. Protects skin and clothing from chemical spills.
Closed Shoes No open-toe sandals. Broken glass is a constant lab floor hazard.
Tie Long Hair Keep away from burner flames and chemicals.
No Eating/Drinking Never consume anything or taste any chemical.
Smelling Chemicals Waft fumes gently toward nose — never inhale directly.
No Mouth Pipetting ALWAYS use a rubber suction bulb — never mouth.

Report Everything Tell instructor about accidents or spills immediately, no matter how minor.

Housekeeping Rules

Spills: Clean immediately after checking with instructor for hazards.
Chemical handling: Take container to stock chemicals, never bring stock to bench.
No returning chemicals: NEVER return unused chemicals to stock bottles — discard.
Read labels: Always verify you have the correct chemical.
Sink disposal: Never pour chemicals into sink without authorization.
⚠️
Emergency Equipment — locate before every session: Eyewash fountain, Safety shower, Fire extinguisher, Fire blanket, First aid kit, All emergency exits.

Experiment 2 — Mass & Volume Measurements

Density, accuracy vs. precision, and proper reading of graduated glassware

Key Formulas

d = m / V
g/mL for liquids, g/cm³ for solids. Rearranged: m = d×V or V = m/d
% Error = |True − Experimental| / True × 100%
Transfer pipet: |10.00 − V_average| / 10.00 × 100%
SD = √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n−1)]
Standard deviation — measures precision
%RSD = SD / Mean × 100%
% Relative standard deviation (precision)

Key Concepts

Increments: Determine the value of each scale division before measuring.
Rule of Thumb: Estimate ONE digit beyond the smallest marked graduation.
Accuracy: How close to the true value — measured by % error.
Precision: Reproducibility of repeated measurements — measured by standard deviation.
Same Balance: Use the same balance throughout — eliminates systematic error.
Temperature Equilibration: Let water reach room temperature before measuring — density is temperature-dependent.

Quick Check

What's the difference between accuracy and precision?
Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value (% error); precision is how reproducible repeated measurements are (standard deviation), regardless of whether they're close to the true value.
Why use the same balance throughout an experiment?
Different balances may have slightly different calibration offsets; using one balance keeps any systematic error constant and cancels out in relative calculations.

Experiment 3 — Nomenclature & Formula Writing

Naming the five classes of inorganic compounds and writing formulas with the criss-cross rule

Five Types of Inorganic Compounds

Type 1 Binary Ionic (fixed charge): Metal (Group IA, IIA, Al, Ga, In, Zn, Cd, Ag) + non-metal. Name: [Metal] + [anion root] + -ide. Ex: CaCl₂ = Calcium chloride, Na₂O = Sodium oxide.
Type 2 Binary Ionic (variable charge): Transition metal + non-metal. Cation charge = (anion charge × anion subscript) ÷ cation subscript. Name: Metal + (Roman numeral) + -ide. Ex: FeCl₃ = Iron(III) chloride, PbO₂ = Lead(IV) oxide. Classical: -ous = lower charge (Fe²⁺ Ferrous, Cu⁺ Cuprous), -ic = higher charge (Fe³⁺ Ferric, Cu²⁺ Cupric).
Polyatomic Metal + polyatomic anion: Use parentheses when subscript > 1. Ex: (NH₄)₂SO₄ = Ammonium sulfate, Fe(NO₃)₃ = Iron(III) nitrate.
Type 3 Molecular (covalent): Two non-metals, Greek prefixes; mono- dropped for first element only. Ex: N₂O₅ = Dinitrogen pentoxide, CCl₄ = Carbon tetrachloride.
Binary Acid H⁺ + non-metal in water (aq). Name: hydro + [root] + ic acid. Ex: HCl(aq) = Hydrochloric acid, HBr(aq) = Hydrobromic acid.
Oxyacid H⁺ + oxygen-containing polyatomic anion. -ate → ic acid; -ite → ous acid. Ex: H₂SO₄ = Sulfuric acid, H₂SO₃ = Sulfurous acid, HNO₃ = Nitric acid, HNO₂ = Nitrous acid.

Criss-Cross Rule Steps

1Write cation first, then anion with charges as superscripts
→
2Swap charge magnitudes as subscripts (criss-cross)
→
3Subscript 1 is NEVER written
→
4Parentheses around polyatomic ions if subscript > 1
→
5Net charge of compound must equal zero
Ca²⁺ + Cl⁻ → CaCl₂
Na⁺ + O²⁻ → Na₂O
NH₄⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → (NH₄)₂SO₄

Experiment 4 — Synthesis of Alum

Recovering aluminum from a beverage can to synthesize potassium alum crystals

Overview

AlK(SO₄)₂·12H₂O
Molar mass = 474.4 g/mol
Starting material
Aluminum metal from a beverage can or foil (~0.5–0.6 g)
1 mol Al → 1 mol alum
1:1 stoichiometry

7-Step Procedure

1Sand Al can with steel wool → cut into ~0.5×0.5 cm pieces
→
2Add 25 mL 1.5M KOH in fume hood → heat gently (don't boil) until Al dissolves (~10–15 min); H₂ evolves
→
3Filter hot solution → filtrate should be clear and colorless
4Slowly add 10 mL 9M H₂SO₄ with stirring — generates considerable heat
→
5Ice bath → cool below 5°C with stirring → alum crystals form
→
6Filter on Büchner funnel → wash with two 10 mL methanol portions → air-dry
→
7Transfer to pre-weighed beaker → weigh → calculate % yield

Key Reactions

2Al(s) + 2OH⁻(aq) + 6H₂O(l) → 2Al(OH)₄⁻(aq) + 3H₂(g)
Step 1 redox — Al oxidised (0→+3); water reduced (H: +1→0)
2KOH + H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
Step 2a
2KAl(OH)₄ + 4H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + K₂SO₄ + 8H₂O
Step 2b
2Al(s)+2KOH+4H₂SO₄+10H₂O → 2AlK(SO₄)₂·12H₂O+3H₂(g)
Overall reaction

% Yield Calculation

% Yield = (Actual mass / Theoretical mass) × 100%
Theoretical mass = moles Al × 474.4 g/mol
🧮
Sample calculation: 0.325 g Al → 0.0120 mol Al → theoretical = 0.0120 × 474.4 = 5.69 g

Key Points

  • Why methanol wash? Alum is insoluble in methanol → removes excess H₂SO₄/K₂SO₄ without dissolving product
  • Why slow cooling? Produces larger, purer, more beautiful crystals
  • Why filter while hot? Prevents early crystallization of impurities in the beaker
  • Drying: Crystals must be completely dry before weighing — wet crystals give falsely high yield

Safety

⚠️
KOH and H₂SO₄ are highly corrosive — wear goggles and gloves at all times.
⚠️
Adding H₂SO₄ to KOH releases considerable heat — add slowly with stirring.
⚠️
Methanol is toxic if swallowed and highly flammable — NO open flames.
⚠️
H₂ gas is flammable — keep fume hood on during Step 1.

Uses of Alum

Water purification (coagulant) Cosmetics & personal care Food preservatives Tanning & leather Dyeing & textiles

Experiment 6 — Redox Reactions & Metal Activity Series

Determining relative reactivity of metals through displacement reactions

Core Definitions

Oxidation = loss of electrons → oxidation number INCREASES
Reduction = gain of electrons → oxidation number DECREASES
Reducing agent = species that is oxidised (donates electrons)
Oxidising agent = species that is reduced (accepts electrons)
💡
OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain  |  LEO goes GER: Lose Electrons Oxidation, Gain Electrons Reduction

Metal Reactivity Series

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Sn > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au > Pt
Most to least active (most to least reactive)

Metal Displacement Rules

  • If metal A reacts with a solution of cation B → A is MORE active than B
  • If no reaction → A is LESS active than B (or equal)
  • Spectator ions (like NO₃⁻) are NEVER included in net ionic equations

Balancing Half-Reactions — Rules

1Write the oxidation half-reaction (species losing e⁻)
→
2Write the reduction half-reaction (species gaining e⁻)
→
3Multiply so electrons lost = electrons gained
→
4Add the two half-reactions — electrons cancel
→
5Check atoms and charge are balanced
Ni(s) → Ni²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
2Ag⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → 2Ag(s)
Ni(s)+2Ag⁺(aq)→Ni²⁺(aq)+2Ag(s)
Ni + Ag⁺ example
Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)
Fe(s)+Cu²⁺(aq)→Fe²⁺(aq)+Cu(s)
Fe is reducing agent; Cu²⁺ is oxidising agent

Experiment 7 — Universal Gas Constant R

Determining R by collecting H₂ gas from the reaction of Mg with HCl

Reaction & Method

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Collect H₂ in an inverted burette over dilute HCl. Measure volume, temperature, and pressure. Calculate R from PV = nRT.

Key Formulas

PV = nRT
R = 0.08206 L·atm/mol·K  or  8.314 J/mol·K
P(H₂) = P_atm − P(H₂O) − ΔP
ΔP (mmHg) = level difference (mm water) ÷ 13.6
V(gas total) = V_dead + (50 mL − final reading)
Dead volume below the 50 mL mark
T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15
1 atm = 760 mmHg → P(atm) = P(mmHg) / 760
R = P(atm)·V(L) / (n·T(K))
n(H₂) = n(Mg) = mass Mg / 24.3 g/mol

Pressure Corrections

ℹ️
Why subtract P(H₂O)? The collected gas is a mixture of H₂ AND water vapour. By Dalton's Law: P(total) = P(H₂) + P(H₂O). Must subtract to get true P(H₂).
📏
Level correction: If water level inside the burette is ABOVE the beaker level → gas pressure is slightly higher than atmospheric → ΔP positive, subtracted. If BELOW → ΔP negative, added.

Statistics (3 Trials Required)

R_avg = (R₁+R₂+R₃)/3
SD = √[Σ(Rᵢ−R_avg)²/(n−1)]
%RSD = (SD/R_avg)×100%
Measures precision
%Error = |R_lit−R_exp|/R_lit×100%
Measures accuracy

Experiment 8 — Thermochemistry: Hess's Law & Heat of Formation of MgO

Combining two calorimetry measurements with Hess's Law to find ΔHf°(MgO)

Core Concepts

Exothermic: ΔH < 0. Heat RELEASED. Temperature RISES. Products have less energy than reactants.
Endothermic: ΔH > 0. Heat ABSORBED. Temperature FALLS. Products have more energy than reactants.
At constant pressure: ΔH_reaction = q_p
1 calorie = 4.184 J. Food Calorie (capital C) = 1 kcal = 1000 cal.
ΔHf°: Heat change forming 1 mole of compound from elements in standard states. ΔHf° = 0 for all elements in standard states.

Styrofoam Cup Calorimeter

q_rxn + q_cal = 0 → q_rxn = −C·ΔT
C = mass of solution (g) × 4.184 J/g·°C
C (200 mL: 50 mL HCl + 150 mL water) = 836.8 J/°C
ΔH_molar = q_p / moles of reactant
Convert J → kJ by dividing by 1000. Density of solution assumed = 1.00 g/mL

Thermogram (Temperature–Time Graph)

  • Record temperature every 30 s for Mg (Part A) and every 10 s for MgO (Part B, reaction is faster)
  • Plot temperature vs time
  • Draw best-fit lines for pre-reaction and post-reaction periods
  • Extrapolate both lines to the time of addition → ΔT = difference between the two lines at that time

Hess's Law & the Three Reactions

ℹ️
Heats of reaction are additive — enthalpy is a state function, path doesn't matter. Reversing a reaction flips the sign of ΔH. Scaling a reaction multiplies ΔH by the same factor.
Reaction 1 (Part A, measured): Mg(s)+2HCl(aq)→MgCl₂(aq)+H₂(g) — ΔH₁
Reaction 2 (Part B, measured): MgO(s)+2HCl(aq)→MgCl₂(aq)+H₂O(l) — ΔH₂
Reaction 3 (known): H₂(g)+½O₂(g)→H₂O(l) — ΔH₃ = −285.6 kJ/mol

Result — ΔHf°(MgO)

Mg(s) + ½O₂(g) → MgO(s)
ΔHf°(MgO) = ΔH₁ − ΔH₂ + ΔH₃
✅
Literature value: −601.7 kJ/mol

How the Combination Works

Reaction 1 (as written): Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂  ΔH₁
Reaction 2 (reversed): MgCl₂ + H₂O → MgO + 2HCl  −ΔH₂
Reaction 3 (as written): H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O  ΔH₃
MgCl₂ and 2HCl cancel → Net: Mg + ½O₂ → MgO
⚠️
Handle conc. HCl in fume hood only. H₂ is flammable — no open flames near the experiment.

Experiment 9 — Spectrophotometric Analysis of KMnO₄

Beer-Lambert Law, building a calibration curve, and finding λ_max

Introduction & Beer-Lambert Law

Spectrophotometry measures light absorbance by a solution to determine its concentration. UV/VIS uses the 200–700 nm range.

A = ε · l · C
A = absorbance (dimensionless) · ε = molar absorptivity (L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹) · l = path length (cm, usually 1) · C = concentration (mol/L). A is directly proportional to C.

Key Relationships

T = I / I₀
Transmittance (fraction of light transmitted)
A = −log(T) = −log(I/I₀)
A increases as T decreases
ε = A / (C · l)
Slope of the A vs C calibration curve
💡
A varies LINEARLY with C. T does NOT vary linearly with C.

KMnO₄ Colour Explanation

  • KMnO₄ absorbs light in the green region (~520–530 nm)
  • Transmits red and blue light → eye sees red + blue = purple
  • λ_max for KMnO₄ ≈ 520–525 nm
  • At λ_max: sensitivity is highest, Beer's Law is most linear, best for quantitative analysis

Stock Solution Preparation

Weigh approximately 126 mg (0.1260 g) solid KMnO₄
Transfer quantitatively to a 100 mL volumetric flask
Fill to the mark with distilled water → concentration ≈ 0.00800 M
Molarity = mass(g) / [158.04 g/mol × 0.1000 L]

Absorption Spectrum Procedure

  • Use Standard 4 (0.0008 M)
  • Set wavelength at 400 nm → zero with distilled water blank → measure A
  • Repeat at 420, 440, 460, 480, 500, 520, 540, 560, 580, 600 nm
  • Plot A vs λ → peak = λ_max

Calibration Curve Procedure

  • Set wavelength at 525 nm (λ_max)
  • Zero with distilled water blank
  • Measure absorbance from MOST DILUTE (Standard 1) to most concentrated (Standard 4)
  • After each measurement, rinse cuvette with the NEXT standard — never with water
  • Plot A (y-axis) vs concentration in mol/L (x-axis); add linear trendline → slope = ε
✅
Key points: Always blank with distilled water before each measurement · rinse cuvette with the NEXT standard between measurements · measure at λ_max for maximum sensitivity · slope of A vs C = ε (when l = 1 cm).

Why is the cuvette rinsed with the next standard instead of water between measurements?
Rinsing with water would dilute the next standard's concentration inside the cuvette, introducing error; rinsing with the next standard itself keeps the concentration accurate.

Experiment 10 — Spectrophotometric Analysis of Aspirin (ASA)

Determining the % active ingredient in an aspirin tablet via the Fe-salicylate complex

Background

  • Aspirin = Acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), molar mass = 180.0 g/mol
  • Effects: analgesic, antipyretic, antiplatelet (low dose)
  • Standard tablet contains 325 mg ASA (regular strength)
  • Tablets also contain inactive ingredients (binders, fillers) — experiment determines % active ASA

Chemistry Method

1ASA + NaOH (heated) → Salicylate anion (SA⁻) + acetate. Hydrolysis of the acetate ester bond
→
2SA⁻ + Fe³⁺ (FeCl₃) → Fe-salicylate complex (violet/purple)
→
3Measure A at 530 nm → compare to SA calibration curve
ℹ️
Why use SA calibration for ASA? Both pure SA and hydrolysed ASA form the IDENTICAL Fe-salicylate complex, so the absorbance per mole of salicylate is the same.

3Transfer to 100.0 mL volumetric flask → fill to mark

Experiment 11 — Volumetric Analysis: Standardisation of NaOH

Titrating NaOH against the primary standard KHP to determine its exact molarity

Background

Titration is a quantitative method to determine concentration. A standard solution (known concentration) is added from a burette to the analyte until the reaction is complete (equivalence point), detected by an indicator color change (endpoint).

ℹ️
Why standardise NaOH? NaOH cannot be a primary standard because it is hygroscopic (absorbs moisture during weighing → unreliable mass) and reacts with atmospheric CO₂ (CO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O → changes concentration).

Primary Standard: KHP

KHC₈H₄O₄  (KHP)
Molar mass = 204.2 g/mol. P = phthalate ion, NOT phosphorus!
KHP + NaOH → KNaP + H₂O
Monoprotic — one ionisable H⁺, reacts 1:1 with NaOH

Four Criteria for a Primary Standard

  • High purity
  • Stable in air during weighing and storage
  • High molar mass (204.2 g/mol → large mass needed → small weighing errors cause small concentration errors)
  • Reacts in a direct, well-defined stoichiometry (1:1 with NaOH)

Indicator: Phenolphthalein

  • Colourless in acidic solution
  • Pink/magenta in basic solution
  • Endpoint = faintest PERSISTENT pink lasting at least 30 seconds
⚠️
Deep pink/magenta = over-titrated (too much NaOH added). Discard and repeat.

Procedure

1Weigh ~0.400 g KHP into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask
→
2Add ~50 mL distilled water → swirl to dissolve
→
3Add 3 drops phenolphthalein
4Fill burette with NaOH (rinse 2–3× with NaOH, not water)
→
5Record initial reading to 0.01 mL
→
6Titrate dropwise while swirling constantly
7Near endpoint: add half-drops → stop at faintest pink persisting 30 s
→
8Record final reading to 0.01 mL
→
9Repeat for 3 trials total

Calculations

V(NaOH) = Final − Initial reading
Moles KHP = mass(g) / 204.2
Moles NaOH = Moles KHP (1:1)
M(NaOH) = Moles NaOH / V(L)
M_avg = (M₁+M₂+M₃)/3
Three molarities must agree within 1% of each other for accurate work

Phenolphthalein Colour Fading

💡
After the endpoint, CO₂ from lab air dissolves into the basic solution: CO₂(g)+H₂O(l)→H₂CO₃(aq), a weak acid that reacts with excess NaOH and lowers the pH, returning the indicator to colourless. This is NORMAL. Breathing into the flask and swirling increases CO₂ → pink briefly returns, confirming the endpoint was correct.
ℹ️
Burette technique: Use both hands — one on the stopcock, one swirling the flask. Read to 0.01 mL. Don't waste time aligning the meniscus exactly at 0.00 mL.

Quick Check

Why can't NaOH be weighed directly and used as a primary standard?
NaOH is hygroscopic (absorbs water from air, making its mass unreliable) and reacts with atmospheric CO₂ to form Na₂CO₃, which changes its effective concentration.
The pink color fades a minute after reaching the endpoint. Should you add more NaOH?
No — this fading is caused by atmospheric CO₂ reacting with the excess NaOH and is normal; it does not mean the endpoint was missed.

🧭 Must-Know Rules — One Per Experiment

1Goggles worn at ALL times; read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
2Estimate one digit beyond the smallest marked graduation; use the same balance throughout.
3Subscript 1 is never written; net charge of a compound must equal zero.
4Filter while hot, cool slowly, wash with methanol — never water — before weighing dry crystals.
5Always use a NEW test tube for each anion group test, in order.
MIdentify only ONE anion per unknown by following the 4-step flowchart in order.
6Spectator ions are never included in net ionic equations.
7Always subtract water vapour pressure from total pressure to get true P(H₂).
8Reversing a reaction flips the sign of ΔH; scaling multiplies ΔH by the same factor.
9Always measure at λ_max and rinse the cuvette with the next standard, never water.
10Use acidified FeCl₃, not water, as the blank — Fe³⁺ itself absorbs at 530 nm.
11NaOH must be standardised against KHP — it is hygroscopic and reacts with CO₂.
